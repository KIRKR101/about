The *Shin-hanga* movement (lit “new prints”) was an art movement in early 20th-century Japan, during the [Taishō](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taish%C5%8D_period "Taishō period") and [Shōwa periods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sh%C5%8Dwa_period "Shōwa period"), that revitalized the traditional _[ukiyo-e](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukiyo-e "Ukiyo-e")_ art rooted in the [Edo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edo_period "Edo period") and [Meiji periods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meiji_period "Meiji period"). It maintained the traditional *ukiyo-e* collaborative system where the artist, carver, printer, and publisher engaged in division of labour, as opposed to the parallel [[Sōsaku-hanga]] (creative prints) movement.

*Shin-hanga* artists incorporated Western elements such as the impression of light and the expression of individual moods. It eschewed *ukiyo-e* traditions of emulating hand-drawn brushstrokes seeking instead to “create works replete with creativity and rich with artistic quality, by avoiding enslavement to hand-drawn painting or old models”.

Watanabe introduced new printing techniques, notably baren *suji-zuri* and *goma-zuri* layering on thicker, drier paper than earlier *ukiyo-e*. He saw *shin-hanga* as fine art (*geijutsu*), distinct from *shinsaku-hanga*, his term for cheaper souvenir prints like [Takahashi Shōtei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Takahashi_Sh%C5%8Dtei)'s. *Shin-hanga* themes stayed traditional; landscapes (_fukeiga_), famous places (_[meishō](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meisho "Meisho")_), beautiful women (_[bijinga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bijinga "Bijinga")_), [kabuki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kabuki "Kabuki") actors (_[yakusha-e](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yakusha-e "Yakusha-e")_), and birds-and-flowers (_[kachō-e](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kach%C5%8D-e "Kachō-e")_).

Shin-hanga was popular with Western collectors, who saw it as nostalgic, romanticised fine art, and exhibitions in Boston (1924), Indianapolis (1926), and Toledo (1930, 1936) — the latter promoted by [Hiroshi Yoshida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hiroshi_Yoshida) — cemented its overseas reputation, with dealers like Robert O. Muller continuing to import prints through the 1930s and postwar. Domestically it fared worse: Meiji-era Westernisation had elevated _yōga_ (Western oil painting) to official status via the [Bunten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan_Fine_Arts_Exhibition#Bunten), leaving shin-hanga dismissed as outdated _ukiyo-e_. As Western demand grew through the 1920s, prints simplified — fewer _baren-suji_ and _goma_ layers, brighter colours — and shin-hanga displaced _shinsaku-hanga_ in the souvenir trade. Wartime militarisation and material rationing from the late 1930s, alongside collapsing overseas markets, brought steep decline. Postwar demand never fully recovered, though artists like [Itō Shinsui](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/It%C5%8D_Shinsui) and Shimura Tatsumi kept the collaborative tradition alive into the 1960s and 1970s, while later decades favoured reproductions and saw _sōsaku-hanga_ eclipse it in prestige. A modest revival emerged in the early 21st century, driven by renewed interest in masters like [Kawase Hasui](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kawase_Hasui) and Yoshida and contemporary artists such as [Paul Binnie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Binnie) — with [Steve Jobs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steve_Jobs) among its notable modern collectors.
